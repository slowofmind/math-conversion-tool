\htitle{Workshop 3}

\begin{workshop}
  Start the workshop with the slide presentation about the testing effect. Basically, taking a test on material has been shown (in many studies, not just this one highlighted in these slides) to lead to more durable learning than just restudying material.

  At Slide 5, give students 5-10 minutes to do the self-test on the last page of this workshop. At some point, stop them and have them do the reflection questions and then turn the self-test in. You don't need to grade these, but if you notice a student who's particularly worrying, please make a note of that with your attendance (in Google Sheets, go to the Insert menu, and select Note).

  Then, talk through Slides 6 - 8. The point here isn't that students must do their homework this way, but to encourage them to think about how they can use the homework for long-term learning (as opposed to just trying to get it done). 

  After that, have students work on the rest of the workshop problems.
\end{workshop}

\item  \label{|h|/h}

  \begin{workshop}
    As the problem says, we'll be using this function tomorrow, so do check that students end up with the right graph.
  \end{workshop}

  Let $g(h) = \dfrac{|h|}{h}$. (This function will be important tomorrow!)

  \begin{enumerate}
  \item

    \begin{problem}[0.25in]
      What's the domain of $g$?
    \end{problem}

    \begin{solution}
      The domain of $g$ is all non-zero $h$, which we also write as
      $(-\infty, 0) \cup (0, \infty)$.
    \end{solution}

  \item

    \begin{problem}[0.25in]
      If $h > 0$, what's a simpler formula for $g(h)$?
    \end{problem}

    \begin{solution}
      When $h > 0$, $|h| = h$, so $g(h) = \dfrac{|h|}{h} = \dfrac{h}{h} = 1$.
    \end{solution}

    \begin{problem}[0.25in]
      If $h < 0$, what's a simpler formula for $g(h)$?
    \end{problem}

    \begin{solution}
      When $h < 0$, $|h| = -h$, so $f(h) = \dfrac{|h|}{h} = \dfrac{-h}{h} = -1$.
    \end{solution}

  \item

    \begin{problem}[\vfill]
      Sketch the graph of $g$. 
    \end{problem}

    \begin{solution}
      \begin{center}
        \begin{tikzpicture}
          \begin{axis}[ytick={-1, 1}, xtick=\empty]
            \addplot+[domain=0:5] {1};
            \addplot+[domain=-5:0] {-1};
            \addplot[open] coordinates{(0,1) (0,-1)};
          \end{axis}
        \end{tikzpicture}
      \end{center}      
    \end{solution}
  \end{enumerate}  

\item  \label{derivative-def-recap}

  \begin{workshop}
    This is to help students really interpret the notation in the definition of the derivative. It's also an opportunity to help students understand that the variable we use in a limit is a ``dummy'' variable. For example, $\displaystyle \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h}$ is the same as $\displaystyle \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \dfrac{f(a + \Delta x) - f(a)}{\Delta x}$ and $\displaystyle \lim_{\diamondsuit \to 0} \dfrac{f(a + \diamondsuit) - f(a)}{\diamondsuit}$.

    If students are bothered by the notation $\Delta x$, reassure them that it's a variable just like $h$ is.
  \end{workshop}

  In each part, you're given two pictures of a graph $y = f(x)$; write formulas for the indicated slopes, using limits for the slopes of tangent lines.

\item  Samantha recently wrote an app and started selling it on the App Store. Let $D(t)$ be the number of downloads her app has in the first $t$ days after she puts it on the App Store.

  Suppose $D(20) = 2000$ and $D'(20) = 150$.
  \begin{enumerate}
  \item
    \begin{problem}[\vfill]
      Interpret the two facts $D(20) = 2000$ and $D'(20) = 150$ in words; include units.
    \end{problem}

  \item
    \begin{problem}[\vfill\newpage]
      Using just the two facts we have, we can make a reasonable approximation of which two of the following quantities? 
      \begin{center}
        $D(19)$ \hspace{1in} $D(22)$ \hspace{1in} $D(155)$
      \end{center}
      Approximate those two, and explain why it isn't reasonable to approximate the other quantity. 
    \end{problem}

  \end{enumerate}

\item  \label{workshop-interpret-and-visualize-derivative} Let $P(t)$ be the population of the world $t$ years after January 1, 2000.

  \begin{enumerate}
  \item \label{workshop-interpret-and-visualize-derivative-interpret}

    Interpret the following quantities (in terms of population and time); include units in your answers.
    \begin{enumerate}
    \item
      \begin{problem}[\vfill]
        $\displaystyle \frac{P(5) - P(2)}{3}$
      \end{problem}

    \item
      \begin{problem}[\vfill]
        $\displaystyle \lim_{x \rightarrow 5} \frac{P(x) -P(5)}{x-5}$
      \end{problem}

    \item
      \begin{problem}[\vfill]
        $\displaystyle \lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\frac{P(2+h) - P(2)}{h}$
      \end{problem}

    \end{enumerate}

  \item
    \begin{problem}[\nstretch{2}]
      If you had a graph of $P(t)$, how would you visualize the 3 quantities in \ref{workshop-interpret-and-visualize-derivative-interpret} on that graph?  
    \end{problem}

  \end{enumerate}
