    \highlight{Formula for goal:} The area of the printed region is 
      $$A = (x-2)(y-4)$$
      To write this as a function of just one variable, we need to \highlight{relate the
      variables using information from the problem.} We're told that the poster has to have an area of 200 square inches. 
      $$xy=200$$
      $$ x = \frac{200}{y}$$
      We can use this to find the area of the printed region as a function of one variable.
      $$A(y) = \left( \frac{200}{y} -2\right ) (y-4) = 200 -2y -\frac{800}{y} +8$$ 
      Considering the margins, the smallest possible value for $y$ is $4$ inches, and
      the smallest possible value for $x$ is $2$ inches. This means that the domain of
      the function is $4 \leq y \leq 100$, or $[4, 100]$.

      \highlight{Critical points:}
      Now we find the critical points. $A'(y)$ is undefined when $y=0$, but this
      isn't in the domain.
      \begin{align*}
      A'(y) &=-2 +\frac{800}{y^2} \\
      0 &= -2 +\frac{800}{y^2} \\
      2y^2 &= 800 \\
      y^2 &= 400 \\
      y &= 20 \\
      \end{align*}
      The only critical point is $y=20$.

      \highlight{Global maximum:} To find the global maximum, we can compare
      the value of $A(y)$ at the critical point and at the endpoints.
      \begin{align*}
      A(4) &= 0  \\
      A(100) &= 0 \\
      A(20) &= 128
      \end{align*}
      This means that the global max occurs at $y=20$. 

      \highlight{Answer the question:} The largest possible printed area is $128$
      square inches.

\item \label{e^-x/x}

  Let $f(x) = \dfrac{e^{-x}}{x}$.

  \begin{enumerate}
  \item \label{e^-x/x-discontinuities}

    \begin{grading}
      2.5 points: 1 for $\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 0^+} f(x)$, 1 for $\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 0^-} f(x)$, and 0.5 for identifying that there's a vertical asymptote at $x = 0$
    \end{grading}

    \begin{problem}[\vfill]
      Find and classify all discontinuities of $f$. Justify your classifications using limits. If a limit does not exist, find both one-sided limits.
    \end{problem}

    \begin{solution}
      Since this function is given by a single formula and its domain is $(-\infty, 0) \cup
      (0, \infty)$, its only discontinuity is at $x=0$. To classify the discontinuity,
      we need to think about $\lim\limits_{x \to 0} f(x)$.

      First, direct substitution gives $\frac{1}{0}$, which indicates we likely have a
      vertical asymptote at $x=0$ and should inspect the one-sided limits. As $x$
      approaches $0$ from the left, the numerator is positive and the denominator is
      negative, so $\lim\limits_{x \to 0^-} \frac{e^{-x}}{x} = -\infty$. As $x$
      approaches $0$ from the right, the numerator is positive and the denominator
      is also positive, so $\lim\limits_{x \to 0^+} \frac{e^{-x}}{x} = \infty$.

      Since the one-sided limits are $\pm \infty$, this confirms that $f$ has a vertical
      asymptote at $x=0$.
    \end{solution}

  \item \label{e^-x/x-limit-to-infinity}

    \begin{grading}
      1 point
    \end{grading}

    \begin{problem}[\stretch{0.5}]
      Find $\displaystyle \lim_{x \to \infty} f(x)$. 
    \end{problem}

    \begin{solution}
      As $x$ goes to infinity, the numerator of $\frac{e^{-x}}{x}$ approaches zero
      and the denominator goes to infinity, so $\lim\limits_{x\to\infty}f(x)=0$.
    \end{solution}

  \item \label{e^-x/x-increasing-decreasing}

    \begin{grading}
      5 points:
      \begin{itemize}[nosep]
      \item 1 for calculating $f'(x)$ correctly
      \item 1 for solving $f'(x) = 0$ correctly
      \item 1 for realizing $x = 0$ also needs to go on the sign chart because $f'(x)$ is undefined at $x = 0$
      \item 1 for testing the regions correctly
      \item 1 for a conclusion that's consistent with their sign chart. Note that here it isn't right to say that $f$ is decreasing on $(-1, \infty)$ because of the vertical asymptote at $x = 0$. If they write that, please deduct 0.5. 
      \end{itemize}
    \end{grading}

    \begin{problem}[\nstretch{1.5}]
      Where is $f$ increasing? Where is $f$ decreasing?
    \end{problem}

    \begin{solution}
      We need to make a sign chart for $f'(x)$. To find $f'(x)$, we use the Quotient Rule:
      \begin{align*}
        f'(x) &= \frac{d}{dx}\!\left(\frac{e^{-x}}{x}\right) \\
        &= \frac{(-e^{-x})(x) - (e^{-x})(1)}{x^2} \\
        &= \frac{-e^{-x}(x+1)}{x^2}
      \end{align*}
      We can see that $f'(x)$ is undefined when $x=0$ and is zero when $x=-1$. Testing
      values gives us the following sign chart.
      \begin{center}
        \begin{tikzpicture}
          \draw[<->] (-3,0) -- (3, 0);
          \node[left] at (-3,0) {$f'(x)$};
          \draw[shift={(-1,0)},color=black] (0pt,3pt) -- (0pt,-3pt) node[below] {$-1$};
          \draw[shift={(1,0)},color=black] (0pt,3pt) -- (0pt,-3pt) node[below] {$0$};
          \node[circle, fill=black, inner sep=2pt] at (-1,0) {};
          \node[circle, draw=black, fill=white, inner sep=2pt] at (1,0) {};
          \node[above] at (-2,0) {$+$};
          \node[above] at (0,0) {$-$};
          \node[above] at (2,0) {$-$};
        \end{tikzpicture}
      \end{center}
      Based on the sign chart, $f$ is increasing on $(-\infty, -1)$, and
      decreasing on $(-1, 0)$ and $(0, \infty)$.
      
    \end{solution}

  \item \label{e^-x/x-max}

    \begin{grading}
      2 points
    \end{grading}

    \begin{problem}[\vfill]
      Does $f$ have a global maximum on $(-\infty, 0)$? If so, what's the global maximum value (the $y$-value), and where is it achieved (at what $x$)? If not, why not? How about a global minimum?
    \end{problem}

    \begin{solution}
      On $(-\infty, 0)$, there is no global minimum because 
      $\lim\limits_{x\to 0^-}f(x)=-\infty$. The global maximum occurs at $x=-1$,
      and the maximum value is $f(-1)=-e$.
    \end{solution}

  \item \label{e^-x/x-limit-to-minus-infinity}

    \begin{grading}
      2 points: 0.5 for the choice, 1.5 for the justification.

      Note: it isn't enough for students to just say that $f$ is increasing on $(-\infty, -1)$, as $f$ could still tend toward a horizontal asymptote as $x \to -\infty$. If this is all they say for their justification, please deduct 1 point. 
    \end{grading}

    \begin{problem}[\vfill]
      One of the following is the value of $\displaystyle \lim_{x \to -\infty} f(x)$. Which one? Explain how you know.
      \[ \infty \qquad 1 \qquad 0 \qquad {-1} \qquad {-\infty}\]
      \emph{Hint:} We don't know enough to calculate $\displaystyle \lim_{x \to -\infty} f(x)$ directly (why not?), but you should be able to use what you've done so far in this problem to eliminate all but 1 choice.
    \end{problem}

    \begin{solution}
      Since we just determined that the global maximum value of $f$ on $(-\infty, 0)$ is
      $-e$, we know that
      $\lim\limits_{x \to -\infty} f(x)$ must be less than $-e$. This eliminates
      all of the choices except $-\infty$.
    \end{solution}

  \begin{problem}[\nstretch{1.5}]
    Let $f(x) = \dfrac{1}{24} - \dfrac{2^x}{3}$. Sketch the graph of $f$, and explain how it's related to the graph of $g(x) = 2^x$. That is, if you start with the graph of $g(x) = 2^x$, what graph transformations could you do to get the graph of $f(x)$? Find and label all asymptotes and intercepts. 
  \end{problem}

  \begin{solution}
    Starting with the graph of $y=2^x$, first we scale vertically by a factor of
    $\frac{1}{3}$ and reflect across the $x$-axis, then we shift up by $\frac{1}{24}$
    units. The horizontal asymptote is $y=\frac{1}{24}$, and the $y$-intercept
    is at $y=\frac{1}{24}-\frac{1}{3}=-\frac{7}{24}$. To find the $x$-intercept,
    we set $f(x)=0$ and solve for $x$.
    \begin{align*}
      \frac{1}{24} - \frac{2^x}{3} &= 0 \\
      \frac{1}{24} &= \frac{2^x}{3} \\
      \frac{1}{8} &= 2^x \\
      x &= -3
    \end{align*}
    The graph looks like this.

        extra y ticks={1/24}, extra y tick labels={$\frac{1}{24}$}]
          \addplot[domain=-5:2] {(1/24)-(2^x/3)};
        \end{axis}
      \end{tikzpicture}
    \end{center}
  \end{solution}
