\item \label{graph-two-runs} 

  One day, Akiko and Bahar jog along the Charles River from Harvard to the Museum of Science, a distance of 9 miles. They leave from Harvard at the same time. Akiko jogs at a constant speed of 6 miles per hour the entire way from Harvard to the Museum of Science. Bahar starts more slowly than Akiko but steadily speeds up throughout her run, passing Akiko after 1 hour.

  Let $A(t)$ be the distance, in miles, that Akiko has run after $t$
  hours. Let $B(t)$ be the distance, in miles, that Bahar has run
  after $t$ hours.

  \begin{enumerate}

  \item

    \begin{grading}
      1 point
    \end{grading}

    \begin{problem}[1in]
      Explain why $B$ is a function of $t$.
    \end{problem}

    \begin{solution}
      For each input (time) $t$, there's exactly 1 output (distance) that Bahar has run.
    \end{solution}

  \item
    \begin{grading}
      6 points.

      Features to look for (1 point each):
      \begin{itemize}[nosep]
      \item They've put time on the horizontal axis and distance on the vertical axis and labeled the axes in some way to make this clear.
      \item Both graphs are increasing.
      \item The graph of $A$ is linear with slope 6.
      \item The graph of $B$ is concave up.
      \item The domain and range of the graphs match what the students say in \ref{graph-two-runs-domain-range}.
      \item On the $t$-axis, 1 and 1.5 should be labeled; on the vertical axis, 9 should be labeled. (They can also label 6 on the vertical axis as the value where $A$ and $B$ cross, but it's okay if they don't.)
      \end{itemize}
    \end{grading}

    \begin{problem}[0.25in]
      Sketch the graphs of both $A(t)$ and $B(t)$ on the axes below. (So both graphs will be on the same set of axes.) Label the axes to say what each represents, and also mark any values you know on the axes. Clearly indicate which graph is $A(t)$ and which is $B(t)$. No explanation is necessary.

      Make sure that you've accurately reflected all of the information above!
    \end{problem}

    \begin{problemonly}
      \axes[xmin=0, ymin=0, enlarge x limits=auto, enlarge y
      limits=auto, width=4in, height=3in]
    \end{problemonly}

    \begin{solution}
      Since Akiko jogs at a constant speed, she runs the same amount in each second. Using the strategy of {{\#3}} on \href{https://people.math.harvard.edu/~jjchen/mathma/docs/worksheets/worksheet01-sols.html}{the worksheet ``Introduction to Functions''}, this means the graph of her distance will be linear, and the slope will be 6 miles per hour. Bahar starts out running more slowly than Akiko but speeds up; using the strategy of {{\#3}} on \href{https://people.math.harvard.edu/~jjchen/mathma/docs/worksheets/worksheet01-sols.html}{the worksheet ``Introduction to Functions''}, this means her graph will be increasing and concave up. Finally, we know that Bahar catches up to Akiko at $t = 1$, so the graphs should cross there. (And, at that time, Akiko has run 6 miles.) 
      \begin{center}
        \begin{tikzpicture}
          \begin{axis}[xtick={1, 1.5}, ytick={6, 9}, xlabel=$t$, ylabel=distance, 
            enlarge x limits=auto]
            \addplot+[domain=0:1.5] {6 * x} node[above] {$A$};
            \addplot+[domain=0:sqrt(1.5)] {6*x^2} node[above] {$B$}; 
          \end{axis}
        \end{tikzpicture}
      \end{center}
    \end{solution}

    \pspace{0.25in}

  \item \label{graph-two-runs-domain-range}

    \begin{grading}
      4 points: 1 for each answer
    \end{grading}

    \begin{problem}[\vfill\newpage]
      What are the domain and range of $A(t)$ and $B(t)$? If you don't have enough information to determine one of these, just write ``not enough info'' and explain why.
    \end{problem}

    \begin{solution}
      Both $A(t)$ and $B(t)$ have range $[0, 9]$ since each jogger runs 9 miles. The domain of $A(t)$ is $[0, 1.5]$ since it takes Akiko 1.5 hours to run the 9 miles. We don't know when Bahar reaches the Museum of Science (except that it's sometime between $t = 1$ and $t = 1.5$), so we don't have enough information to determinate the domain of $B(t)$.
    \end{solution}

  \item

    \begin{grading}
      1 point, 0.5 for identifying that it's linear and 0.5 for identifying that this is because Akiko runs at a constant speed.
    \end{grading}

    \begin{problem}[1in]
      Is $A(t)$ linear, concave up, or concave down? Explain why in terms of the rate that Akiko runs.
    \end{problem}

    \begin{solution}
      Since Akiko runs at a constant speed, $A$ is linear. 
    \end{solution}

  \item

    \begin{grading}
      1 point, graded for consistency with whatever they drew in their graph.
    \end{grading}

    \begin{problem}[\nstretch{0.25}]
      Is $B(t)$ linear, concave up, or concave down? No explanation is necessary.
    \end{problem}

    \begin{solution}
      $B$ is concave up. 
    \end{solution}

  \end{enumerate}

\item \label{cleaning-greenough} 

  Before 2020, Harvard hired students to clean dorms at the end of each summer.

  Let $G(s)$ be the number of hours it would take $s$ students working together to completely clean Greenough. For example, writing $G(100) = 5$ means it would take 100 students working together 5 hours to completely clean Greenough.

  In this problem, you'll model $G(s)$. As is often the case, there isn't a single correct model; rather, the important thing is to communicate how you're interpreting the situation and using your interpretation to create your model.

  \begin{enumerate}
  \item

    \begin{grading}
      2 points.

      They don't have to give the same answer that the solution does; they should get full credit for any choice that has a consistent explanation. So, for instance, they might argue something like ``When there are just a few students, more students will make the work go faster; but once there are too many students, more will just slow it down. So, the graph will decrease, then increase''. That should get full credit.

      But if they misinterpret the situation or write an explanation that disagrees with their choice, then you should take a deduction. 
    \end{grading}

    \begin{problem}[\vfill]
      Do you think $G(s)$ is increasing or decreasing, or does it switch from one to the other? Explain your thinking.
    \end{problem}

    \begin{solution}
      I think that, the more students there are, the less time it will take the students to clean Greenough. (More students should make it go faster.) So, I think $G$ is \fbox{decreasing}.
    \end{solution}

  \item

    \begin{grading}
      2 points. Again, they don't have to give the same answer as the solution, but they should be thinking about how a change in the number of students causes a change in the total number of hours required to clean Greenough, and they should give an answer consistent with what they think about that change.
    \end{grading}

    \begin{problem}[\vfill]
      Do you think $G(s)$ is linear? Explain.
    \end{problem}

    \begin{solution}
      No. I think that increasing from, say, 1 to 2 students would reduce the total time needed to clean Greenough by a lot, while increasing from 100 to 101 students would reduce the total time needed by only a little. So, there isn't a constant rate of change in this situation.
    \end{solution}

  \item

    \begin{grading}
      1 point for properly interpreting what this would mean 
    \end{grading}

    \begin{problem}[\vfill]
      Do you think the graph of $G(s)$ should intersect the horizontal axis? Why or why not?
    \end{problem}

    \begin{solution}
      If the graph intersected the horizontal axis at some value $a$, that would say that $a$ students working together can clean Greenough in 0 hours. That doesn't make sense, so the graph shouldn't intersect the horizontal axis.
    \end{solution}

  \item

    \begin{grading}
      4 points:
      \begin{itemize}[nosep]
      \item 1 for putting students on the horizontal axis, hours on the vertical axis, and labeling the axes so you can tell which variable is which
      \item 3 points for sketching a graph that matches their answers to the 3 previous parts
      \end{itemize}
    \end{grading}

    \begin{problem}[0.1in]
      Sketch what you think the graph of $G(s)$ looks like. Be sure to label your axes to indicate which variable each represents. No additional explanation is necessary.
    \end{problem}

    \begin{problemonly}
      \axes[xmin=0, ymin=0, enlarge x limits=auto, enlarge y
      limits=auto, width=3.6in, height=2.7in]      
    \end{problemonly}

    \begin{solution}
      \begin{center}
        \begin{tikzpicture}
          \begin{axis}[xmin=0, xtick={1}, xlabel=students, ylabel={hours}, 
            ytick=\empty] 
            \addplot+[domain=1:10] {1 / x}; 
          \end{axis}
        \end{tikzpicture}
      \end{center}
    \end{solution}

  \end{enumerate}

\item 

  \emph{In the previous two problems, you went from a verbal description of a function to a graph. We also often want to go from an algebraic formula for a function, like $f(x) = x^2$, to a graph. This is something you've almost certainly done in prior math classes; if you'd like a refresher on how to do this, watch \href{https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2-dUHLHeyTY}{this video}. (Note: If you're doing this on paper, underlined text like this is a link in the PDF, so see the PDF on Canvas for the link.)}

  \begin{enumerate}
  \item

    \begin{grading}
      2 points: 1 for having a line with positive slope, 1 for having reasonable intercepts (a negative $x$-intercept and a positive $y$-intercept)
    \end{grading}

    \begin{problem}[\vfill]
      Sketch the graph of $f(x) = 2x + 1$. No explanation is necessary. 
    \end{problem}

    \begin{solution}
      \begin{center}
        \begin{tikzpicture}
          \begin{axis}[axis equal image, ytick={-5, -4, ..., 5}, width=3in]
            \addplot+[domain=-2.2:2.2] {2*x+1};
          \end{axis}
        \end{tikzpicture}
      \end{center}
    \end{solution}

  \item

    \begin{grading}
      2 points, 0.5 for going through the origin and 1.5 for the right
      general shape 
    \end{grading}

    \begin{problem}[\vfill]
      Sketch the graph of $g(x) = x^2$. No explanation is necessary. 
    \end{problem}

    \begin{solution}
      \begin{center}
        \begin{tikzpicture}
          \begin{axis}[axis equal image, ytick={-5, -4, ..., 5}, width=3in]
            \addplot+[domain=-2.2:2.2] {x^2};
          \end{axis}
        \end{tikzpicture}
      \end{center}
    \end{solution}

  \end{enumerate}
